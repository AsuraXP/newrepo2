You never imagined the safest place in the world would be your grandfather's farm. But when the world ends, it's the first place you run.

That morning begins like any other. You wake up in your own bed, stretch beneath the blanket, and stare at the ceiling. There is no warning in the quiet. No hint that, by nightfall, the roads will be filled with people running for their lives.

You make coffee, settle onto the couch, and open a book. The television murmurs in the background. You scroll through your phone, consider going back to sleep, and take another sip.

Then the program cuts out.

A news anchor appears, speaking too quickly. Behind her, a city street is drowned in smoke. People sprint past abandoned cars. Figures stumble after them, moving in strange, jerky bursts. The camera shakes, and for a moment, you think it must be a movie.

It isn't.

The anchor says attacks are spreading across the country. The infected are gathering in massive swarms. Police lines have collapsed. The army has been called in, but even they cannot contain them. The picture switches to a highway, where bodies lie among the wreckage, piling up as smoke rises above the traffic.

You reach for your phone. Every notification tells the same story. Sirens begin to sound outside your window. Somewhere nearby, someone screams.

Your coffee slips from your hand and spills across the floor.

You lock the door, pull the curtains shut, and hurry to the kitchen. A few cans of food. Half a loaf of bread. Bottled water. You count everything twice. If you are careful, you have enough to last three days.

For a moment, you breathe again. Three days is something. Maybe help will come. Maybe the power will stay on, and someone will figure this out.

Then you look back at the television. The anchor is gone. The screen shows an emergency message, followed by static.

What happens on the fourth day?

You stand in the middle of the kitchen, listening to the sirens grow closer. You could stay and hope. You could follow the crowds, though the roads already look impossible. Your hands tremble as you try to think.

And then you remember the farm.

Your grandparents live beyond the edge of town, where the paved roads give way to fields. Your grandfather built the place to stand on its own. You used to laugh when he explained every wire, every water tank, every row of vegetables. Now you remember his voice with painful clarity.

The solar panels keep the lights on. A well supplies water. Chickens provide eggs, and the garden feeds the family. There are fences, tools, stores of food, and a barn built strong enough to outlast a storm.

You grab your backpack. Water, canned food, a flashlight, a jacket. You take a small first aid kit and the old paper map your grandfather once insisted you keep. Before leaving, you try calling him. Then your grandmother. Neither answers.

The silence on the line makes your stomach tighten. You tell yourself they are busy. They have to be.

Outside, smoke curls between buildings. A car alarm wails. You hurry down the street, keeping close to the walls. At the intersection, a crowd pushes past you, fleeing a swarm that spills around the corner. You duck into an alley and wait, holding your breath as footsteps thunder by.

When the noise fades, you run.

Your car is still where you left it. You climb in, turn the key, and pull away. But the main road is jammed with abandoned vehicles. Far ahead, a column of smoke rises over the highway. You check the map and turn onto a narrow side street.

The farther you travel, the quieter it becomes. Houses thin out. Fields replace storefronts. Once, you spot a lone figure in the road and steer around it without stopping. Your fuel gauge sinks toward empty, but the familiar hills finally appear in the distance.

Then the engine sputters.

You coast to the roadside, grab your bag, and continue on foot. Your legs ache. The sky turns orange, then gray. Every rustle in the tall grass makes you turn. You keep walking anyway, following the fence line your grandfather taught you to recognize.

At last, you see the windmill above the trees.

The farm gate is closed. Beyond it, the solar panels catch the last of the light. Chickens shuffle in their enclosure. A goat watches from beside the barn, and neat rows of vegetables stretch across the garden. The place is still standing. For the first time all day, hope feels real.

You open the gate and hurry toward the house. You call for your grandparents. No one answers.

The front door is unlocked. Inside, a lamp glows softly. Your grandmother's favorite cup sits on the table beside a folded note. Your hands shake as you open it.

Your grandmother is gone. The note says she died before help could reach her. Your grandfather buried her beneath the old apple tree. He asks whoever finds the note to look after the animals and keep the farm going.

A sound comes from the next room.

You turn. Your grandfather stands in the doorway, clothes torn, eyes vacant. He takes an unsteady step toward you, and you understand. He has been infected.

You back away slowly, tears blurring the room. He was the one you came here to find. Now you cannot bring him back. You slip outside and close the door between you, securing it as gently as you can.

Beyond the fence, darkness settles over the fields. The animals still need water. The garden will need tending. Tomorrow, you will have to decide what to do next.

Tonight, you stand beneath the apple tree and say goodbye. The farm your grandfather built has given you a chance to survive. Holding on to it will be up to you.
