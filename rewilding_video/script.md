You wake before the radio does, the way you always do, to the sound of wind moving through a forest that used to be a suburb.

You lie in the bunk and listen to it. Pine. Oak. Wind in the power lines that stopped carrying power forty years before you were born. Somewhere below the ridge, a highway unspools under moss and wild grape, straight as a scar that healed crooked. You know every sound this station makes at dawn. The settling beams. The water pump. The long, low groan of the antenna mast, leaning a degree further every year into the gray morning.

Then the radio hisses, and the day begins the way every day begins.

"Sector Seven, this is Base." A woman's voice, flat with coffee and distance. "Say your count."

You lift the handset from its hook. "Sector Seven. Morning one, in position. Cameras armed on the crossing. I'll do the foot count at midday."

"Log it," Base says, "and remember, if you lose visual, you do not pursue. You log and you withdraw."

"Copy," you say, the way you always say it, and Base clicks off, and you lie there one second longer, because there is something you have not told Base yet.

There is a calf.

You found the tracks two days ago, in the mud by the creek crossing. Small. Bare. The gait of a toddler who never learned to walk and somehow did. And beside them, in the same mud, the long-toed, dragging print of an adult. One adult. Walking slow. Bleeding nothing, because they do not bleed anymore. Going somewhere with terrible patience.

You logged the adult prints. You did not log the small ones. You told yourself it was a rounding error. You told yourself a lot of things, those two days.

The Collapse was fifty years ago, and nobody alive remembers the world that ended. You know it the way you know scripture: the fever that was never quite a fever, the streets that filled in a single bad month, the long years when the cities emptied and the Army walked patrols that came back fewer every time. You know the enclaves on the coasts, with their walls and their clinics and their rules. You know the Authority. And you know the thing the Authority does not say on the brochures: the turned did not burn out. They adapted. They thinned. They settled into the deep green like deer into a meadow, and they move through it on a path older than roads.

They call it the Drift. The herds follow water and season, the way elk do. They do not hunt anymore. They do not seek. They migrate, generation after silent generation, and nobody can fully explain how they persist, only that they do, because they always have.

Your job is to count them.

You are a ranger of the Rewilding Authority, Third Meridian, Sector Seven. You carry a dart gun you have never fired in anger, a pair of binoculars older than your parents, and a manual with more addenda than rules. You collar nothing. You tag nothing permanent. You count, you photograph, you note condition and direction, and every month Base flies a drone over the crossing and checks your numbers, and the numbers go into a ledger in a tower in a walled city by the sea, because knowing where the herds are is the difference between a safe world and a lucky one.

You make coffee on the camp stove. You check the camera traps, one by one, spooling the film in the dry little dark of your pack. You tell yourself what you always tell yourself. You are a clerk. A clerk of the wilderness. Clerks do not interfere.

At the second camera, you find the thing you feared.

The frame is timestamped 04:17. Night grain, infrared white and green. In it, the herd crosses the creek bed — eleven adults, unmistakable even at that distance, that hitching, tidelike gait — and behind the last of them, so low to the ground she is nearly a shadow, the adult you logged two days ago. And under her chest, tucked against her, a small bright shape. Wrapped in something. A tarp scrap. A jacket. A piece of the old world, remade into a sling.

The calf.

You sit down in the leaf mold with the photograph in your hands, and for a long time you do not move.

The manual has a page for a turned animal found dead. It has a page for a new settlement found in the Drift. It does not have a page for this, because this has never happened, because this cannot happen, because turners do not have children, because the world that ended took the children first, and because — and your mind keeps returning to this, over and over — someone had to carry that baby into the deep woods and leave it there, or lose it, or die, and the herd found it, and the herd kept it.

You zoom the photo until the pixels break. The adult's head is bowed. Not browsing. Bowed. The posture of something standing guard over warmth.

You do not radio Base.

You tell yourself it is because Base would send a team, and the team would do what teams do, and the ledger would close over this like water over a stone. You tell yourself a dozen other things. The truth is simpler. You have spent nine years cataloguing things that cannot be helped, and you are so tired, so tired, and this is the first thing in nine years that might be helped.

You follow the herd.

It is not hard. They are not hiding. They move at the pace of growing things, east along the old rail line, into the upper valley where the fog never quite lifts and the trees grow so close their trunks touch. You keep to the ridge. You watch through the binoculars. You log nothing.

On the second day you see it with your own eyes, through the glasses, from three hundred yards.

The calf walking. Swaying, toddling, furious, alive. A girl, maybe two years old, wrapped in a flannel jacket too big for anyone living. The herd walks around her the way a river moves around a stone. The turners do not touch her. They do not seem to see her as food, or as threat, or as anything. They see her the way geese see a borrowed gosling. A thing in the middle of the flock that must be moved, kept, carried, and not lost.

You lower the binoculars and your hands are shaking.

The river is rising. The upstream dam failed in the spring, everyone knows it, Base reminds everyone every week. The crossing the herd is making for is a quarter mile of open water between this bank and the far hills. At the pace they move, they will reach it tomorrow. And the calf cannot swim. And the herd does not know what swimming is, or what drowning is, or what the word tomorrow means.

You have a dart gun. You have a radio. You have a rule.

You climb down from the ridge.

You do not aim the gun. You never even draw it. You walk into the open with your hands loose at your sides, and the herd stops. Eleven heads come around. That tidal gait halts. The forest goes so quiet you can hear the creek. And then the small shape in the flannel jacket turns, and looks at you with eyes that have never known a wall, and does the one thing nobody has done at you in nine years.

She smiles.

You are fifty yards closer. Thirty. You do not run. You do not shout. You talk, the way the manual says you must never talk in the field — you talk like a person, because you are a person and she should learn it. You tell her your name. You tell her the sky is real. You tell her the water is coming up and you are going to help her over.

The herd watches. The herd does not advance. The herd does not flee. They turn, slowly, all of them, the way they turn at the season, and they begin — not toward you, not away from you — they begin toward the high ground, up the rocky spine the old survey maps call Hawk's Back, where the water cannot reach.

And you understand, standing there in the cold with your heart hammering, that they are not attacking. They are not retreating. They are leading.

The calf does not understand roads. She understands the herd. She takes three unsteady steps after them, up the shale. And you — you sit down in the mud, and you laugh, and you cry, and you do not follow, because some part of you, the part that has been a clerk for nine years, knows you cannot walk a child out of the wilderness into a world that has a page missing.

You climb back to the ridge. You watch them cross Hawk's Back as the water takes the low ground behind them. You watch the flannel jacket disappear into the fog above the timberline, eleven shadows and one small light, all walking the same tide-road east.

That evening you key the radio. Your voice is very calm.

"Base. Sector Seven. Count complete at the crossing. Eleven confirmed, heading northeast, condition good."

A pause. "Eleven? Last month you logged eleven."

"Confirmed," you say. "Same eleven."

Another pause, longer. Then: "Copy, Sector Seven. Ledger updated. Base out."

You sit on the porch of the station as the dark comes down, and the first stars come out over a country that forgot itself and grew back anyway, green over concrete, deer in the parking lots, trumpet vine up the overpasses, and somewhere east of here, walking, a child who belongs to the last wild thing that remembers how to keep a calf.

You do not know her name. You are her ranger. You will come back in the spring.

Because that is what the Rewilding Authority never got around to printing on the brochures: the wild does not decide what is broken. It only decides what survives. And sometimes, walking the slow tide-road through the green, survival looks like eleven of the dead, and one living girl in a stolen jacket, and a man on a ridge pretending very hard not to cry.

Your radio clicks. Base, one more time. "Sector Seven. Confirm — same eleven?"

You look at the eastern hills. The fog moves over them like a breath.

"Same eleven," you say.

And you leave the channel open, in case she ever needs to hear a human voice say her own name back to her.
