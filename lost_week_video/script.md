# SCRIPT — "THE WEEK YOU LOST"

POV: second person ("you"), present tense
Target runtime: 10:00
Voice: en-US-AndrewNeural @ +10% (Edge-TTS, one continuous take, word boundaries as the master clock)

Note: Lines beginning with `##` are structural act markers. They are NOT narrated.

## ACT I — THE SEVEN DAYS

You wake up, and the light is wrong.

Flat and white and far too bright, the way a room looks when you have slept long enough to forget it.

Your mouth is dry. There is a taste in it you cannot name. Copper, maybe. The bottom of a coin.

You lie still and listen. The building is quiet. Not traffic-and-neighbors quiet. The radiator is silent. The fridge is silent. The air feels like it is holding something back.

And then your stomach turns over.

Not hungry. Ravenous. The kind of hunger that has hands. It drags you out of bed, and you do not even look at your phone, because eating is the only thought you can hold.

You eat standing up. Cold rice from the pot. Half a loaf of bread. Something in a container with a date you do not read. You do not taste it. You keep going until the shaking stops.

That is when you pick up your phone.

Forty-seven missed calls.

Two hundred and eleven messages.

And the date. Read it again. Read it slower.

It is seven days later than it should be.

You sit down on the kitchen floor, back against the cabinet, and something in your chest goes very quiet.

Because a week ago, you remember this clearly, you were coming home late.

The stairwell smelled like rain and cigarettes. A man came up past you. Nothing you would have remembered later. Except his mouth was wet. Except he was making a sound like he was trying to clear something that would not clear.

He lunged. You shoved him back, hard, the way you would shove anyone.

And he caught your arm.

You felt it. A ring of pressure just below the elbow. Not a bite, you told yourself. A scrape. A bad one, but a scrape.

You pushed him off. He fell. He stayed down.

You looked at him on the concrete and thought: he looks fine. He looks like a man who slipped. Nothing in him you could point to and call dangerous.

So you went home. You washed your arm. You put on a bandage. You went to sleep.

And you did not wake up for seven days.

You roll up your sleeve.

The bandage is gone. The skin underneath is whole. Smooth. Pale. No scab. No scar. Nothing.

And your stomach growls again. Deep. Patient. Patient as a dog waiting under a table.

## ACT II — THE EVIDENCE

You start looking for the week.

The fridge is empty. Not empty like you skipped groceries. Empty like something cleaned it out. The bin is full of packaging you do not remember opening, and the bag is fresh.

Your shoes are by the door, caked in mud from a street that is not yours. The laces are double-knotted. You never double-knot. Your keys are in the sink, under a glass of water you do not remember pouring.

The door chain hangs loose. Not broken. Cut, and hung back up so it would look the way you left it. You tell yourself it was you. Who else would it be.

Then your phone rings, and it is your sister, and you almost answer. But you look at the call log first, and you see that she has called every day this week, and you see what she left in your voicemail four days ago.

Your own voice.

You do not remember recording it.

You press play.

"You should not come over. If I called you and you are hearing this, do not come over. I am not — " and it stops. Not the message. The recording. Nine seconds in, like the phone was put down.

You play it again. The words are fine. The breathing is not. Too slow. Too even. Like something that does not need the air, but has learned to make the sound.

Outside, the street is doing nothing. A car sits at the curb with its door open and its engine running, and nobody is walking past it to care.

You turn on the television. You leave it on for six minutes.

There is a word they are using. Not the word from the films. A quieter word. The word is "stage."

Stage one is a bad fever that lasts about a week.

Stage two is when the fever breaks and the person stops being a person.

Stage three is when they stand up.

You sit there with the television talking to an empty room, and you understand, very calmly, that you never had stage one.

You had a nap.

You go to the mirror and look at yourself the way you would at a stranger. Two eyes. Your own haircut, badly grown out. Then you count what is wrong.

Your pupils do not move when the light changes. Your heart: thirty-one beats a minute. Then twenty-eight. Then under twenty, a number that belongs to animals in winter.

You smell the neighbor through the wall. Not cooking. Something under it, warm and sweet and open, like a cut left out.

You have never smelled a person through a wall before.

## ACT III — THE HUNGER

You test it, because the alternative is sitting still.

You let the last thing in the freezer thaw and eat it standing up, raw, and feel nothing. Not disgust. Not appetite. Just the hunger being fed, the way you would pour water into a plant.

You feel patient.

That night, you lose time.

You come back standing in the hallway outside your door at four in the morning, hand still raised like you were about to knock. Grit on your palms. A warm ache across your knuckles, the kind you get from hitting something that hit back.

Down the hall, the neighbor's door is open two inches.

You do not go in. You lie in bed until the light comes, telling yourself a story about sleeplessness.

The next night you set your phone on the dresser and record yourself.

You watch the footage the way you would watch someone else's home movies.

For forty minutes you sit on the edge of the bed, hands on your knees, perfectly still. Then you stand. You do not stretch. You do not check the time. You walk out of frame. The room stays empty for four hours and eleven minutes. Then you walk back in and sit down in the same spot.

The timestamp jumps. Your hair is wet in the later footage. Your sleeve is torn at the cuff.

Somewhere in those four hours, you leaned into the camera. You did not look at it. You looked past it, at the door. And you said, slowly, like a man reading a note he did not write:

"You are not asleep. You are busy."

You stop the video. You sit in the dark and think about how many nights there have been.

You take a knife from the drawer and press the tip into the old bite mark, just enough to open the skin.

It does not bleed the way it should. It bleeds like a photograph of blood. While you watch, the edges of the cut pull together, the way water closes over a dropped stone.

You are not healing. You are being repaired.

And the hunger sits up inside you and says, in a voice with no words in it: not bread. Not rice. Not this.

## ACT IV — THE CARRIER

Here is what you learn, and it takes you most of the next day to say it out loud.

The bite does not make everyone the same thing.

Some people get the fever, burn through it, stand up, and are gone. That is stage three. That is the shape on the street with the open car door.

But some people get the bite and nothing happens. No fever. No dreams. Just a week that goes missing, then a hunger, then a body that comes back a little more repaired each time it is damaged.

Those people do not turn.

Those people carry.

You are one of those people.

That is why the man looked normal. Why the building is quiet. Why the neighbor's door is open two inches, and why there is a smear on the stairwell wall that nobody has come to clean up.

You did not sleep for seven days. You were out. You were working. You were the thing that walks, except you kept your face and your name and just enough of yourself to be horrified afterward.

That is the whole trick of it. That is the design.

You are not afraid of the bite. The bite was never the danger. The danger is the week you cannot remember, the hand that keeps doing things without asking you, the way your own voice on a recording sounds so calm when it tells you not to come closer.

Your sister calls again. You let it ring twice. Then you answer.

She is coming. The roads are bad but she is coming, forty minutes away, and she has been trying to reach you for a week.

You say: do not come.

She says: I am not leaving you in there.

You say: I am not in here.

She is quiet. Then she says your name the way she said it when you were children, and something behind your ribs moves that has nothing to do with hunger.

You tell her to bring a padlock. Not for the front door. For the inside of it.

She asks why.

You say: because I do not want to be the reason.

## ACT V — THE DOOR

She comes. She stands at the end of the hallway with groceries and a padlock, and does not come closer, because you are in the doorway with your arms out, palms open, the way you would stand before a fire.

You talk for eleven minutes. The stairwell. The bandage that was gone. The footage, the four hours, the neighbor's door.

She asks the only question that matters.

She asks: are you still in there.

You think about it honestly, which is the last human thing you get to do.

And you say: enough of me is.

She slides the padlock across the floor. You pick it up. You close the door. You put the lock through the inside bracket, where it will hold against the door opening from within. You hear her hands on the other side of the wood, then her steps, then nothing.

You sit down with your back against the door.

The hunger sits down beside you. It is patient. It has all night. It has every night.

Outside, the street is very quiet, and the city is a shape in the window, and somewhere down there a car is still running with its door open.

You take a breath you do not need. You say her name out loud.

Then again. Then the date, and the colour of the kitchen when you were six, and the street you grew up on. You say the name of the man in the stairwell, though you never learned it, because naming him is a thing a person does.

The dead do not do this. That is all dying is. Forgetting, with a body left standing.

So you keep talking. You keep the hallway light on. You keep the padlock shut, and her name in your mouth like a stone you refuse to swallow.

You are not afraid of the bite.

You are the bite.

And the only part of you left that still counts is the part that keeps the door closed.
