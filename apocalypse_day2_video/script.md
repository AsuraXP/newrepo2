Five forty five in the morning. Pale grey dawn slips through the cracked roof vent of the mountain fire lookout cabin across the wool blanket on your shoulders. Your throat feels dry as chalk. Unzipping your dark backpack on the floorboards, you shake your last plastic water bottle. Barely two swallows slosh at the bottom. Steep climbs and dry oatmeal yesterday burned through your water twice as fast as you planned. Rising with your steel crowbar, you unlatch one wooden shutter an inch and peer down through dusty brass binoculars hanging beside the map table. Your stomach drops. Down on the logging road, the abandoned camper van is surrounded. Overnight, an endless tide of hundreds of pale infected spilled out of the dead city and now marches steadily uphill into the pines. Staying in this dry wooden box means dying of thirst while that wave cuts off every trail below. Day One was about surviving the panic, and Day Two hands you a harsher test, find clean water and push north before the mountain closes around you.

You pack your blanket, check the duct taped magazine armor under your jacket sleeves, open the deck trapdoor, and bolt the wooden boarding ladder back into place. Cold mist clings to the pines as you descend and hike north along a narrow deer path. Twenty minutes later, rushing water pulls you to a clear mountain stream tumbling between granite boulders. Parched, you kneel on the gravel bank, unscrew your empty bottle, and dip its rim toward the current. Half an inch above the surface, you freeze. Torn grey fabric snags on a branch thirty yards upstream. Creeping around the bend, you stop cold. Two pale infected hikers from last night lie wedged motionless between fallen logs in the shallow rapids, tainting every drop flowing downstream. One sip of bad water with every hospital dark will end your run just like a bite. Backing off, you climb eighty yards up the cliffside until ice cold spring water seeps straight from a mossy granite crack, filling every bottle pure at the source.

By nine fifteen, following the high ridge leads you to a fenced clearing holding a green roofed forestry ranger station and a chain link equipment yard. Parked inside sits an olive utility quad bike with a heavy cargo rack, nimble enough for narrow trails where cars cannot pass. As you crouch behind a pine trunk, three sharp metallic taps ring from the station roof. Beside a solar panel crouches a woman in a faded blue paramedic jacket with dark tied back hair, waving urgently and pointing down at the porch. Below her, three crooked infected in torn flannel scrape at the splintering office door, drawn by a weather radio squawking static inside. Walking away leaves a medic to die and leaves three infected guarding the keys to that quad.

Fighting three infected head on is a losing gamble, so you let noise do the work. Circling forty yards downwind along the fence, you hurl a heavy river stone hard against the hollow metal bed of a rusted pickup down the slope. The loud clang echoes across the clearing. Instantly, all three pale figures turn from the porch and shamble down toward the truck. You vault the porch rail, slip inside the dim office, click off the radio, grab the quad keys and a trail map off the wall board, and pop the ceiling ladder hatch. The medic drops lightly down with a red trauma bag slung across her chest. Catching her breath, she whispers her name is Maya, a county paramedic carrying water tablets, sutures, and word of a fenced hydroelectric dam twelve miles north across Twin Creek Gorge.

Together you unlock the gate, strap both packs onto the olive quad, and ride dirt fire roads north under pale afternoon sun. For two hours the ridges stay clear, until the trail narrows between sheer rock walls above Twin Creek Gorge. Rounding a cliff bend at two thirty, you slam the brakes. Spanning the whitewater canyon ahead hangs an old wooden footbridge, and a fresh rockslide has smashed a five foot gap straight through the planks near the far cliff, way too wide for four wheels. Worse, echoing up the narrow trail behind you comes a chorus of rasping throats and dragging boots. Three dozen infected pushed uphill from the highway round the bend two hundred yards back, pinning you and Maya against an eighty foot drop.

Leaping a five foot gap with heavy packs is one slip from death. You take one steady breath and look under the quad's rear cargo rack, where two heavy perforated steel loading ramps sit bolted in place. Working fast with Maya, you unpin both steel ramps, grab your packs and crowbar, sprint onto the swaying bridge, and lay both steel ramps side by side across the shattered planks. Maya crosses first, drops to her knees on the far rock ledge, and braces the steel ends with both hands. You sprint across right behind her just as the lead infected step onto the near bridge approach. Spinning around on safe rock, you and Maya yank both steel ramps back onto your side, reopening the sheer five foot chasm and stopping the shambling crowd cold at the broken edge. As amber dusk settles over the canyon, you share clean water on the north ridge while the distant concrete wall of the mountain dam gleams beside dark calm water ahead. Your bottles are full, you have someone watching your back, and you just survived Day Two.
