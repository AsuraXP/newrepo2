# 72 Hours After the Zombie Breakout

POV: Second person ("you"). Fictional outbreak, real survival logic.
Voice: en-US-AndrewNeural at +10 percent (Edge-TTS, Kaggle only).
Runtime target: 8 minutes, about 3.1 words per second.

<!-- TTS:START -->
Seventy two hours. That is how long a city needs to go from traffic to silence.

Not because the dead are fast. Because everything that keeps you alive runs on things that stop.

Water pressure. Refrigeration. Garbage collection. The phone in your pocket. The neighbour who has a key to the building.

You get three days before the easy version of your life is over. Here is what actually kills people in those three days. It is almost never the dead.

This is fiction. Treat it as a drill.

Hour zero to hour three. One decision, and it is the only one that matters. Stay where you are, or move once, now, and then stay put for good.

Most people freeze, then move at the worst possible time, in a car, on a road, with everyone else.

If your building is solid and you have water, stay. If your building is glass, or one room, or full of strangers you would not trust with your keys, leave in the first hour, while the streets are still confused and nobody has decided anything yet.

Move on foot. A car is a metal box that stops working when the road stops working, and the road always stops working.

Take one bag. Water, a knife, a lighter, a flashlight, a phone, a charger, a coat, and a paper map. Nothing you would cry about losing.

Then stop moving. Every hour you spend travelling is an hour you cannot spend making where you are safe.

One more thing, and it is uncomfortable. The people who become a problem are already inside the building. Count your neighbours now, while they are still just neighbours. You are not making a list of enemies. You are making a list of doors.

Hour three to hour twelve. Water. Not weapons. Water.

A person dies of thirst in about three days, and stress makes you drink more, and fear makes you sweat, and the tap will not run forever.

So while the water still runs, fill everything. The bathtub. Buckets. Jugs. Every bottle with a lid. Fill them first, before the bag, before the plan, before the phone call. Every hour after this, someone else in your building is doing the same thing, and the pressure is going down for all of you.

Three liters per person per day is the baseline for drinking. Plan four. Cooking and washing come out of the same number.

A full bath is roughly eighty liters. Twenty days of drinking for one person, or five days for four. Now you know why the bath matters and the television does not.

Do not drink from a swimming pool. Do not drink from a radiator. Do not drink from the toilet tank unless there is no chemical block in it, and there probably is.

And if the tap stops before you finish filling, you can still make water safe. Boil it for one minute. Or put two drops of plain unscented bleach into a liter, stir it, cover it, and leave it for thirty minutes. Not the splashless kind. Not the scented kind. Plain.

Fill the kettle. Fill the coffee pot. Fill the bottle at the back of the cupboard that you never use. Fill it now, while it still makes sense to.

Hour twelve to hour twenty four. The first night is a light problem.

A light is visible for kilometers. A candle in a window is a sign with an arrow on it, pointing at your door. The dead may not care. The living absolutely do.

So close the curtains before you need them. Use one room, one low light, and keep it below the window line. Tape over the little lights on the router and the television, because a dark house with three glowing dots is still a lit house.

Your phone is a tool and a beacon. Airplane mode saves the battery and stops the radio from introducing you to the street. Download the map of your own city before you need it, because the one thing nobody memorizes any more is how to walk home. Then put the phone down. A screen at night is a small blue lamp with a face behind it.

Do not build a fire indoors. Not for warmth, not for cooking, not for comfort. Carbon monoxide kills quietly in a sealed room, and nobody finds you in time.

Sleep in shifts if there are two of you. Six hours total, in two blocks, so that someone is always awake and listening.

Listening matters more than watching. Sound gives you distance, direction, and numbers. Eyesight gives you a shape, and then a panic.

Hour twenty four to hour forty eight. Now the house becomes a machine.

You need heat, water, food, waste, and silence, in that order. Pick the smallest room you can actually heat, and live in it.

Close the doors you are not using. Block the ones you are, but not with furniture stacked to the ceiling. Use one heavy thing, placed so that it makes a noise when it moves, so you hear a problem before you have to solve it.

Your waste has to go somewhere. A bucket with a lid, lined with a bag, kept away from your water and your food. This is not glamorous. It is the thing that puts four people in a hospital when there are no hospitals, and sickness in a sealed house is worse than anything outside it.

Eat the food that spoils first. A closed refrigerator stays cold for about four hours, and a freezer lasts about two days if it is full, one if it is nearly empty. So the fridge is a four hour countdown and the freezer is a two day one. After that you are living on cans, and the good meals are memories.

You will want to clear the building. Do not. Every corridor you clear is a corridor you have to clear again tomorrow, and the noise you make doing it is free information for anything that hears you. Secure your floor. Learn your stairwell. Leave the rest.

Do not cook smells. Onions, bacon, coffee. A smell travels further than a sound, and it tells everyone exactly what it is.

And wash your hands. A cut from a can lid in a world with no antibiotics is a serious injury. That is the unglamorous truth of the first two days. The dead are loud and slow and obvious. Bacteria are quiet.

Hour forty eight to hour seventy two. Other people.

By day three, the ones who were going to panic have panicked. The ones who were going to help have not arrived. What is left is small groups who have worked out that survival is a shared resource, and they will be careful about who they share it with.

Trust is a currency this week, and there is not much of it in circulation. Spend it slowly.

Watch hands. Watch how someone stands at your door. Watch whether they keep looking past you while they talk to you.

Meet people in the street, never inside your house, and never after dark. Keep a door between you and them, and keep the conversation short.

Three to five people is a group. Two is a partnership, and it breaks when one of them gets tired. Ten is a crowd, and a crowd needs a leader, and in the first week the leader is usually just the loudest person in the room.

Do not shout for rescue. Do not wave from a roof unless you can see the helicopter and the helicopter can see you. A signal is free and it is advertised. Everyone within a kilometer learns where you slept.

Help does not come at seventy two hours. Aid does, sometimes, early, and only to fixed public places. If you go, go once, go light, go with a way back, and go knowing that the crowd at the aid point is the most dangerous crowd left in the city.

And if you are bitten, know the rule before you need it. There is nothing in the house that changes it. Say what you need to say from a doorway, and do not make somebody else do it for you.

That is the hard one. Everything before it was just water and curtains.

Day three ends strangely. You are alive, and nothing you did was brave.

You filled a bath. You sat in the dark. You ate cold beans and stayed quiet while the street made noises you chose not to investigate.

That is the whole job. Survival in the first seventy two hours is not a fight. It is a list, done quietly, in the right order, while you still have a choice about the order.

Learn the list now. In the week where nothing happens, it costs you a bathtub of water and one evening of planning. In the week where it happens, it is the difference between being a person with a plan, and being the reason nobody opens the door.
