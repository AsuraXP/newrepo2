- Formula: What/How/Why + "Ancient Humans" + a familiar modern experience or an

  delivery and unsourced sensationalism. Prefers 8-12 minutes.
- Replicability is high: animation plus narration, no personality bottleneck.

## The gap worth taking (the twist)

Every hit answers "what was it like to be them" from the outside. None of the
top performers takes the viewer *inside a single bad day* and then explains a
piece of science the viewer did not know they were getting. That is the twist:
one continuous second-person ordeal (a body-level emergency) that keeps cutting
to real evidence, ending on a reverse of the channel's own "terrifying animal"
framing.

## Candidate topics

### A. "What Did Ancient Humans Do When Someone Broke a Leg?" — RECOMMENDED

- Twist: the first invention was not fire, the wheel or the spear. It was the
  stretcher. The channel sells ancient humans as terrifying predators; this
  flips it — the terrifying moment is being the one who cannot run.
- Story (second person): a hunt goes wrong, your shin snaps, the band has to
  decide in one afternoon whether carrying you is worth the animals it costs.
- The new thing explained: bone healing as a clock readable in fossils, the
  bioarchaeology-of-care method, and the live academic fight over whether healed
  trauma proves compassion or only tolerance.
- Evidence: Shanidar 1 (crushed left orbit, withered/amputated right forearm,
  deafness from auditory exostoses, arthritis, healed metatarsal fracture; died
  at ~40-50 after years of survival) [homininhistory.com/blog/shanidar-neanderthals];
  Trinkaus & Villotte 2017 in PLOS ONE; Spikins et al. 2018, World Archaeology,
  on Neanderthal health care (fever management, hygiene, repositioning)
  [earth.com/science/neanderthals-compassionate/]; the sceptical counter-reading
  (passive tolerance, reciprocal/kin support, and the disability-expertise
  critique that survival is not proof of compassion)
  [researchgate.net/publication/392687247].
- Caution: the "Margaret Mead: a healed femur is the first sign of civilization"
  anecdote is apocryphal — if used, it must be labelled as a story, not a finding.
- Runtime fit: 8 minutes.

### B. "How Did Ancient Humans Survive a Broken Tooth?"

- Twist: the first dentist was not a dentist. Someone held your head and put a
  sharpened flint into your jaw.
- Story: a molar cracks on grit, the pain spreads to your ear, and the band's
  answer is either pull it, drill it, or leave you behind.
- New thing explained: why worn-flat ancient teeth were often cavity-free until
  farming, then caries exploded; early direct intervention in the archaeological
  record; pain management without anaesthesia.
- Risk: several claims in this space circulate as viral factoids and need primary
  sourcing before scripting. Verification required in State 2.
- Runtime fit: 7 minutes.

### C. "What Did Ancient Humans Do When They Couldn't Sleep?"

- Twist: nobody slept alone, and nobody slept unguarded. Insomnia was a job.
- Story: you draw the middle watch, everyone else is down, and something moves
  past the firelight.
- New thing explained: the sentinel hypothesis, one-hemisphere-at-a-time sleep,
  and the "first night effect" that still wakes you in a strange room.
- Risk: mostly animal-model evidence; must be labelled as inference, not
  prehistoric fact.
- Runtime fit: 7 minutes.
