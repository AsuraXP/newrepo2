Seven twelve in the morning. You are slouched on your slate blue couch with a yellow blanket over your knees, scrolling half awake through your phone while the wall television mutters across the room. A red banner crawls beneath a reporter talking about a riot downtown and sudden hospital lockouts. You reach for your coffee mug, thinking it is just another Tuesday. Then three frantic knocks rattle the door across the hall. Heavy slams shake the wall. You cross barefoot and press one eye to your peephole. Under the flickering bulb, your neighbor Marcus sways with a torn sleeve and chalk white skin, begging Mrs. Miller in four B to open up because somebody bit him on the train. Before you can shout, Mrs. Miller slides her chain latch back. Marcus lunges inside with a ragged snarl, the door slams shut, and muffled screaming echoes down the hall. Your blood turns to ice as every piece clicks together at once. This is not a riot, and it is not the flu. You are standing at ground zero of a full blown catastrophe, a real zombie apocalypse straight out of the movies you watched growing up, and Day One just reached your floor.

Every nerve in your body screams to yank your lock open and bolt down the hall in your socks. You grip the doorframe and force yourself to breathe for five seconds. Running blind puts you face to face with whatever just happened in four B. Before touching that knob, you sprint to the bathroom, jam the plug into the tub, crank the cold tap open in case you get pinned inside, and fill two liter bottles at the sink. Next, you pull on heavy jeans, lace thick boots, and shrug into a canvas jacket. Remembering Marcus's torn sleeve, you grab two thick glossy magazines off the table and duct tape them tight around both forearms beneath your jacket, instant bite armor built in forty seconds. You sweep a flashlight, lighter, gauze, and protein bars into your backpack, grab a steel crowbar from the closet, pocket your car keys, and press your ear flat against the front door.

Out in the hall, four B has gone quiet except for a slow thump against the inside of its door. You turn your deadbolt millimeter by millimeter so it never clicks, ease the door open, and step out with your crowbar raised. At the far end, silver elevator doors chime and slide open. Your upstairs neighbor Dan stands inside with two huge suitcases, waving and hissing for you to jump in. You take two steps forward, glance at the flickering bulb overhead, and freeze. When a city panics, power grids fail without warning. You shake your head, whisper for Dan to get out, and push through the heavy fire stair door instead. Dan rolls his eyes and hits the lobby button. Ten seconds later, halfway down the concrete stairs, the lights stutter twice and snap pitch dark. Behind the concrete wall beside you, elevator cables groan to a violent stop, trapping Dan inside a dead steel box between floors.

You shoulder open the ground exit bar into harsh morning sunlight and stop cold on the steps. Black smoke rises behind three downtown towers while sirens wail from every direction. Across your parking lot, terrified neighbors hurl suitcases into trunks, smash bumpers, and funnel left onto Oak Avenue toward the Interstate ninety ramp. You sprint to your grey sedan, fire the engine, and roll to the curb, ready to follow the crowd out of town. Then you rise in your seat and look three blocks ahead. Nothing on the highway ramp is moving. A jackknifed fuel tanker blocks all four lanes in thick black smoke, locking thousands of cars bumper to bumper. Worse, at the back of that jam, twitching figures pour out of a subway stairwell, yanking on car handles while trapped drivers flee on foot. Instead of turning left into that steel trap, you spin the wheel hard right, hop a low curb, and speed down an empty industrial alley away from the highway.

An hour later, back streets carry you to the warehouse district, yet two problems stare from your dashboard. Your gas needle sits near empty, and three protein bars will not last past tomorrow. One block over on Main Boulevard, a supermarket lot looks like a battlefield. Hundreds of panicked shoppers shove through shattered glass doors, fighting over carts while an empty police car flashes blue lights into the smoke. Pulling in there is suicide. You coast past and scan a dull brick strip two streets back until a quiet building catches your eye, a tax office beside a tiny vet clinic. Nobody loots accountants on Day One. You park in the empty back alley, kill the engine, and listen for sixty seconds. Dead silent. Using your crowbar tip, you pop the clinic latch and pack bandages, antiseptic, and saline in two minutes. Next door, untouched office breakroom cupboards hand you bottled water, oatmeal packets, trail mix, and peanut butter without a fight.

By two in the afternoon, radio stations dissolve into static, cell bars vanish, and your engine sputters onto fumes where suburbs meet wooded hills. You coast onto the cracked concrete of a tiny two pump gas station, hoping for a fuel can. The pump screens are dead black. Before your tires stop, the side door bangs open and two desperate men step out swinging aluminum bats. The taller man points his bat at your windshield and shouts to step out slow, drop your pack, and hand over the keys. Fighting two armed men when every hospital is dark means one swing breaks your arm and ends your run. You raise both hands, step out keeping your pack straps on, hold the keys high, and hurl them twenty feet left into tall weeds. Both men dive into the grass after their prize, never knowing the tank is dry. Before they look up, you vault a wooden fence behind the station and vanish into the pines.

Four thirty brings cold mountain wind through the trees as you hike uphill beside an overgrown logging road, staying twenty yards inside the timber. Behind you, grey smoke blankets the valley floor. Your legs burn, tempting you to crawl inside an abandoned white camper van parked beside a creek culvert to sleep. You stop ten paces back and study the mud around its tires. Fresh footprints crowd the gravel, the side window is cracked, and that road is the main funnel every straggler will walk tonight. You bypass the easy trap and climb half a mile up a rocky switchback until heavy timber posts rise above the pines, an old forestry fire lookout cabin standing thirty feet up on wooden stilts. One narrow staircase leads to a deck with a hinged trapdoor. You climb slowly, sweep the one room cabin clear with your crowbar, and find a dusty bunk, storm shutters, and an iron stove. Stepping back outside, you unbolt the lower boarding ladder, haul it onto the deck, and drop the trapdoor shut.

Dusk swallows the ridge by seven, plunging the forest into freezing darkness with zero city glow left alive. Shivering in your jacket, you kneel beside the iron stove, stack dry pine twigs inside, and pull the lighter from your pocket. Warmth feels irresistible. Your thumb touches the spark wheel when a tiny orange pinpoint flares half a mile down in the valley. You freeze and peek through a crack in the wooden shutter. Down in a roadside clearing, another group of survivors built a bright campfire to warm their hands, lighting the surrounding trees like a stage. Within minutes, pale, jerky silhouettes emerge silently from three patches of dark woods, drawn straight toward that glowing flame and drifting smoke. Distant shouts echo up the hillside, a flashlight spins wildly across the trunks, and then the clearing falls horribly quiet while the fire burns alone. You slip your unlit lighter into your pocket, latch every shutter tight, wrap an old wool blanket around your shoulders, and chew cold oatmeal in darkness.

Eleven fifty eight at night. You lie flat on the floorboards inside the elevated cabin, breathing quietly through your nose in pitch darkness. Thirty feet below on the forest floor, dry pine needles crunch under slow, dragging footsteps. One shape, then two more shuffle out of the black trees right beneath your stilts. You grip your steel crowbar against your chest and do not move a muscle. Down in the dirt, they circle the posts twice, scraping at bare wood where the lower ladder hung. No smoke rises from your chimney, no light leaks through your shutters, and no board creaks overhead. Far down near the highway, a stuck car horn blares into the night, and one by one the footsteps below turn away toward the sound. Five minutes later, you crack a roof vent two inches toward the freezing sky. Thousands of bright white stars blaze across deep midnight blue above the pines. Your watch clicks past twelve. You just survived Day One.
