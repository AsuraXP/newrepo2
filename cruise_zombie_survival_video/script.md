Your vacation begins three hours after the world starts ending.

The trip was meant to celebrate Lina's thirteenth birthday. You saved for a year to afford an inside cabin, then spent months watching her research the ship's waterslides and dessert bars. Now the same terminal screens that once advertised destinations flash emergency warnings. Every departure board shows cancellations except yours.

You stand beneath the white hull of the Meridian, clutching your boarding pass while sirens wail somewhere beyond the cruise terminal. Around you, thousands of passengers drag suitcases toward the gangway. Nobody wants to admit why everyone is suddenly so eager to leave land.

Your younger sister, Lina, pulls your red backpack from your shoulder and checks the zipper for the third time. Inside are two water bottles, a flashlight, and the snacks she insisted on bringing. You tease her for packing like a doomsday prepper. She does not laugh.

Lina is small for thirteen, with two dark braids tucked beneath a purple hoodie. Your green windbreaker is decorated with a cheap enamel anchor she pinned above the zipper that morning. She said it would make you look like an experienced sailor.

A crew member clips a yellow band around your wrist and directs you aboard. Behind you, a family is refused entry after security finds something wrong during their medical screening. Their shouting follows you into the terminal, then disappears beneath the ship's welcoming music.

The band is printed with your muster station and cabin number. You photograph the emergency route beside the gangway, just in case. Lina rolls her eyes and says you have been watching too much news.

The atrium looks untouched by the panic outside. A glass elevator glides past balconies. Servers carry trays of drinks. A pianist plays beside a shining sculpture. For one strange moment, the disaster feels like a show you have already left.

Fourteen decks rise above you, linked by glass elevators and curved staircases. The ship holds restaurants, a theater, a clinic, and enough cabins to feel like a city that accidentally learned to float.

Then the ship's horn sounds, deep enough to shake the windows.

You and Lina join the crowd along the promenade railing. The Meridian slides away from the pier. On shore, people are running between abandoned cars. Farther inland, black smoke rises above the city. As open water widens between you and the port, passengers begin to cheer.

Below the railing, lifeboats hang in orange rows. You remember the safety card in your cabin and count the jackets in a storage locker. For all the ship's luxury, its safety equipment suddenly interests you more than the pools.

You do not cheer. You watch the land until it becomes a thin, burning line.

The captain announces that the ship will remain at sea until authorities declare the ports safe. His voice stays calm. He describes the situation as a temporary precaution. He asks everyone to enjoy lunch.

In the buffet, Lina finally smiles. She fills a plate with fruit and cake while you find a table beside the windows. For several minutes, the cruise becomes ordinary again.

Lina describes the cake she plans to try every evening. You make a list on a napkin, deliberately giving her a future beyond this afternoon. Outside, a Coast Guard vessel crosses the wake, but neither of you mentions it.

Then an elderly passenger near the dessert station drops his tray.

A crew member kneels to help him. The man jerks upright, pale and shivering, with cloudy eyes fixed on her hands. You see a dark stain beneath his collar. Before you can call out, he lunges.

Lina's fork strikes the floor.

The room erupts. Chairs topple. A tray crashes beside your foot. You grab Lina and pull her behind a serving counter as passengers rush toward the same narrow exit. The fallen crew member does not get up. The old man turns toward the nearest running child.

You throw a metal pitcher at his shoulder. It connects just long enough for the child's mother to drag her away. Then you and Lina run.

Outside, the atrium is chaos. People pound on elevator buttons. Others crowd the grand staircase, blocking anyone trying to climb. Overhead, the captain repeats an instruction to return to your cabins, but the cabin elevators open onto another scream.

A young deck officer with a cropped black braid forces her way against the crowd. Her name tag reads Mara.

Up close, she looks only a few years older than you. A silver radio is clipped to her white uniform, and a ring of brass keys hangs from her belt. She blows a whistle and points toward a blue crew door. She shouts that the central stairwell is compromised and the upper decks can still be isolated.

You take Lina's hand and follow Mara.

The service corridor beyond the door is narrow, bright, and suddenly quiet. A dozen passengers stumble inside before another officer seals it. Through the steel, you hear fists striking the other side. Then the fists stop, replaced by a slow scraping sound.

Mara leads you upward. At each landing, more crew members report through her radio. An infected passenger has been found on deck five. Two medical staff are missing. The lower cabins are no longer secure. The captain has ordered everyone to shelter in place, but nobody knows which cabins are safe.

Mara explains that fire doors can divide the ship into sealed sections, but only if nobody props them open for missing relatives. She orders two passengers to guard each landing. You notice that she gives every frightened person a specific job.

You reach the pool deck under a sky still pretending this is a perfect afternoon. Abandoned towels hang from loungers. A plastic cup rolls across the tiles. Beyond the rail, there is nothing but blue water. The wind carries salt instead of smoke. For once, the emptiness beyond the rail feels like protection instead of isolation. For the first time since boarding, you understand the ship's one enormous advantage.

Nothing on land can reach you without first crossing the sea.

Mara gathers the survivors beside the forward observation lounge. Its steel shutters can be closed manually, and the crew galley behind it holds drinking water. She needs three volunteers to help bring passengers from the aft stairs before the shutters come down.

You volunteer before fear can talk you out of it.

You think of the child in the buffet and the family refused at the terminal. Staying behind a locked door would be easier. It would also leave strangers facing the same terror you escaped. Lina catches your sleeve. You tell her to stay inside, but she lifts the flashlight from your backpack and says she is coming too. Mara gives you both one sharp look, then hands Lina a spare radio.

The three of you follow the outer walkway toward the stern. Wind snaps at your clothes. Every doorway looks like a mouth waiting to open.

Your shoes slip on salt water. Lina keeps the radio against her chest while Mara checks each corner before waving you forward.

At the aft stairs, you find twenty passengers trapped between a locked crew gate above and a growing crowd below.

They are not pounding on the gate because they want to escape. They are pounding because infected passengers have entered the stairwell beneath them.

Mara unlocks the gate. You wave the survivors through while Lina shines her light down the steps. A man carrying a toddler nearly falls. You catch his elbow and pull him onto the walkway. Behind him, gray hands curl around the railing.

There is no time to be quiet anymore.

You run as the gate crashes open. The infected spill onto the deck with the same terrible, uneven speed you saw in the buffet. Passengers scatter around the pool. One woman freezes in front of you, unable to choose between the lounge and the stairs. You seize her wrist and drag her with you.

Halfway back, Lina's radio slips from her hand. It skids beneath a lounger. She stops for half a second, and that is enough for a man in a torn cabin robe to lunge between you.

You swing the red backpack into his chest. He staggers against the rail, but another figure emerges behind him. Lina freezes beneath a white canopy, her flashlight rolling away across the deck.

You hear Mara shouting your names.

Instead of backing away, you kick a wheeled drinks cart with both feet. It rolls across the wet tiles and strikes the lounger beside Lina. The lounger topples between her and the reaching figures. She ducks under the canopy and runs. You catch her hand as Mara reaches your side.

Together, you sprint through the lounge entrance.

The first steel shutter descends behind you. The second jams halfway, leaving a gap just wide enough for a person to crawl through. On the other side, shadows stumble past the outdoor tables.

Mara drops beside the control box and tears off its cover. You brace your shoulder against the shutter while Lina holds the flashlight steady. The mechanism shakes above you. Fingers appear beneath the bottom edge.

Mara reconnects a loose cable and slams the manual switch. The shutter jolts downward. You jump back as it seals against the floor with a final metallic crack.

For several seconds, nobody speaks. Then the toddler you helped begins to cry, and the whole room seems to breathe again.

Someone passes around towels. Another passenger finds a first-aid kit. Lina's hands are shaking so badly that you wrap both of yours around them until the flashlight beam stops dancing.

By midnight, the crew has secured the upper decks. The infected remain below, separated by steel fire doors and locked service gates. Nobody calls the situation under control. There are injuries to treat, cabins to search, and frightened people who still have not found their families.

Volunteers inventory bottled water in the galley. A retired nurse organizes a quiet corner for the injured. Cooks cover abandoned buffet trays and carry food upward in sealed carts. Nobody knows how long the upper decks must remain isolated, so every meal is counted and every faucet is checked.

You sit with Lina on the observation lounge floor, sharing the crushed snacks from your backpack. Mara brings you both water. Her uniform is torn at one sleeve, and her calm expression has finally disappeared. She looks as tired as you feel.

Her radio crackles. The bridge reports that the ship is holding position far outside the shipping lanes. The ports are still closed. Smoke covers most of the coastline. For now, the Meridian has fuel, food, fresh water, and enough distance from land.

The bridge has restored contact with two other ships, both turned away from their destinations. Their captains have agreed to share weather reports and remain offshore. For the first time, the silence on the radio feels less like abandonment.

At dawn, you step onto a small secured balcony with Lina. The city you left is invisible beyond the horizon. Behind you, survivors are learning names, counting supplies, and turning a floating hotel into something none of you planned for.

Someone has drawn a crude map of the safe decks and taped it beside the galley. Children sleep under tablecloths. Crew members take turns at the shutters, listening for movement below.

Lina asks where the ship will go next.

You look at the open water, then at the yellow band still circling your wrist. Yesterday, it meant you were a passenger on a seven-day cruise. Now it means you belong to one small group of people who made it through the night.

You tell her you are staying away from shore.

The Meridian carries no promise of rescue. It cannot keep the world outside forever. But there are locks on the doors, water in the tanks, and people working beside you. Below the balcony, the sea moves against the hull in long, steady waves.

For the first time since the sirens began, you let yourself believe that you might survive.
