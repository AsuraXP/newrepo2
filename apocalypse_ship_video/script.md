A zombie bit you last week — and you punched him in the face, knocked him out cold, and went home to sleep. Yeah, sounds like the dumbest way to die in human history. Except you didn't die. Seven days later, you woke up still human. Everything you're about to see — every bit of it — happens in one single day. Day one. Let me rewind.

All you wanted was bed. Double shift, feet screaming, too many drinks at the bar — nobody was counting — and a two-block walk through dead-quiet streets, the good kind of quiet, streetlights humming, nothing open but your eyelids. Then footsteps came up fast behind you. You glanced back. Some guy crossing the road toward you. Jacket, jeans, ordinary. The kind of man you'd ignore on any other night. He mumbled something you didn't catch, sped up, got way inside your space — and his teeth closed around your arm.

You screamed. He tasted wrong — like biting into cold meat left out too long. His eyes were glassy, his skin like a refrigerator — but his face? Normal. Totally normal. Drunk-normal. And your tired, tipsy brain did the math and got zero. You didn't get scared. You got angry. You shoved him back — he stumbled, raised his head, came at you again — and that's when you swung. One clean punch. He hit the pavement like a sack of potatoes and just… stayed there. Froth at the corner of his mouth. Not moving. Somewhere above you a window slammed shut, and you weren't about to check who watched. You waited one second for him to get up swinging. He didn't. So you checked your arm — two red marks, a little blood — and thought: I'll deal with this tomorrow.

You didn't deal with it tomorrow. Because for you, tomorrow didn't arrive for seven whole days.

The next thing you know, your skull is pounding like a bass drum. Sunlight is slicing through the curtains. You crawl to the charger, plug in your phone — dead so long it takes forever to breathe — and when it wakes, the messages crawl in like a funeral procession. Missed calls from mom. From your boss. Three numbers you don't even know. And the street outside sounds wrong. Horns, nonstop, no rhythm. Under everything: growling. Deep, wet, endless.

You drag yourself to the window and pull the curtain back.

Your street is packed. Not rush-hour packed — swarm packed. Bodies shoulder to shoulder, moving together like one slow river. First guess: protest. Then you look closer. Half of them have no hands. Some are missing pieces you can't unsee — things hanging that shouldn't hang. And don't look at the pavement. Don't. Cars stopped dead both ways, doors open, interiors empty. Your brain files an official report: still dreaming, still hungover, go back to bed.

But your arm itches. You scratch it — and there they are. Two faded marks. Completely healed. Normal scratches don't disappear in a week. And on the way to the window you passed your front door: beside the handle, deep and fresh, are scratches. Something tried to get in while you slept. Seven days of them. You slept through every one. And somehow you're still here.

So you do what anyone would do. You turn on the TV. Every channel wears the same red banner. Outbreak. Collapse. Cities falling, borders shut, armies losing. It is not one city. It is everywhere. Then they show YOUR city. Your intersection. Your street — shot from a helicopter, your building inside the frame. You are watching your own apocalypse in high definition, and it does not look like a dream. The earliest panic messages are dated six days ago — the world found out six days ago. You got bitten the night BEFORE that. Whatever he was… he was early. And the anchor keeps repeating two words: stay indoors. You're standing in your boxers with a bite you can't explain, and the world ended while you slept.

And then the memory hits. The street. The glassy eyes. The punch. He wasn't drunk. He was one of them — and he put his mouth on you. So why aren't you out there with no hands and bad intentions? Why didn't you turn? There is exactly one way to find out.

You open your front door. And there is one. Right on your landing. Shoulder against your railing. It turns to face you. You stop breathing. It doesn't. You wave a hand in front of its dead eyes — nothing. One step closer — no flinch. You get within arm's reach of a zombie, and it treats you like furniture. You could run. You could scream. Instead, a stupid grin splits your face. You're not prey. You're not bait. You're immune. The only person on the planet who can walk out that door and come back with groceries. On the way back up with a bag of rice you brush past three more on the stairs — and you realize you've stopped holding your breath.

Take a breath. Because the next thought knocks the grin right off.

If anybody ever finds out what you are, you're finished. Not zombie-finished. Human-finished. A cage, a hundred needles, a file with your name on it. You have watched enough movies to know how this story goes: the cure doesn't get a statue — it gets a cell. They would lock you down, hunt you down, hold you down, and take and take until what's left of you is paperwork. Your freedom — gone. Your life — gone. You would never see a sunrise you weren't scheduled for.

And here's the sneaky thought that proves it. You didn't turn. That means something in your blood beats this thing. Maybe you're the cure. Maybe your blood could save the whole screaming planet. Nice thought, right? Wrong. Because a cure is only a cure to people who need it — and everyone on this dying earth needs it from you. So you take that thought, you put it in a drawer, and you bury the drawer. Rule one, starting now: be normal. Never flinch. Never mention it. Nobody — not survivors, not soldiers, not your own mother — ever finds out. The most valuable person alive is also the most hunted person alive, and you haven't even had coffee yet.

So you spend the rest of day one learning the new rules. Outside your door: a buffet of man-eaters you're the only customer they can't touch. You move through them like a ghost. They don't sniff. They don't snap. You are invisible — and it gets ridiculous fast. You're reaching for supplies in a ransacked store, zombies shuffling past your shoulders, you grab the last can of beans and say sorry out of habit — and some groaning thing bumps your cart like you're in its way. You drive a car straight through a jammed intersection, horn dead, and the swarm parts around you like water around a stone. By midafternoon you're cocky enough to fall in with them — shoulders down, slow shuffle, just another body in the current — and for ten minutes you're part of the river. Nobody shoots. Nobody asks for papers. The dead are safer company than the living.

Because the dead aren't your problem anymore. Humans are.

The survivors on your block already are. They've made camp at the end of the street — armed, exhausted, terrified, and shooting first. One afternoon they spot you: no weapon, no cover, strolling between the dead like you're late for work. Guns up. Who the hell ARE you? You play dumb — lucky route, adrenaline, didn't see the big one — and mostly they buy it. Mostly. Then it happens: one of them breaks from cover twenty meters out, a biter closing fast on his heels — and it runs straight past you. Straight past. Not a glance. The one who's been watching you goes quiet. It ran right past you, he says. You don't blink. It ran past everyone. He doesn't look convinced. Nobody does. You slip away before dark — and rule two gets written twice in your head: hide it from the living harder than you hide it from the dead. Because zombies bite. Humans interrogate. And as you round the corner, you see the watcher lift a radio to his mouth. You don't hear what he says. You don't have to.

Back home, you do the most human thing left in the world. You make dinner. You do the dishes. The apocalypse doesn't do dishes — you do. And while the kettle boils, the loop starts, and it won't stop. Why you? Was it the bite — did that night rewrite you? Or were you always different? You never catch colds. You heal weird fast. That broken wrist at fifteen — three weeks instead of three months. Maybe it was always in your blood. Maybe it was the three drinks. Maybe poor life choices are the real vaccine. You don't know. And you can't ask anyone. The only one who could answer is the guy face-down on your street — so you check from the window. He's not there anymore. Just a dent in the asphalt where you put him down. And a smear that ends.

But you are not the only one who changed. Because all day, the dead have been doing something new. They don't attack you — they watch you. Every window you pass, heads turning slow, tracking you like a searchlight. You step outside at noon and every head on the block snaps toward you in one motion, like a wave. And now — at your own window — the shuffling outside stops the second you lean to the glass. Like an audience settling when the actor arrives.

At three in the morning you hear it in the hallway: shuffling. Then nothing. You crack your door open. They're lined up — motionless, all facing you. They aren't clawing. They aren't drooling. They're just present — and now the scratches beside your handle make sense. They weren't trying to get in. They were checking you were home. You close the door without a sound. Your hands are shaking. Through the window, across the street, one figure stands apart from the rest. Jacket. Jeans. Ordinary. Not swaying with the others. Facing your window. He remembers you.

Now count your problems. One: the living. The man with the radio at the end of your block saw you stroll untouched through the dead — and he's already talked. They don't know your name. Yet. Two: the dead. No longer food — now an audience, in your hallway, under your window, all facing your door. You are the only immune human alive. The most valuable person on the planet. The most hunted person on the planet. And you still haven't told a single soul.

Here's the truth nobody wants to hear: the bite wasn't the end of anything. It was the interview. The world kept going after it — only now the world has plans for you. So keep your head down, keep your mouth shut, keep breathing.

Day one — done. You survived it. The secret's buried, the cure's in the drawer, and you're still human. Somewhere in the dark, the man you punched is standing at your window, and at the end of the block a radio is describing you word for word.

Day two starts when they knock. And they will knock. Because zombies don't knock…

…humans do.

And the dead are still waiting for you to run — or to wake all the way up.
