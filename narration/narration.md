Imagine you wake in Rome around the year two hundred, in a rented upstairs room above a street filling with the newly dead.
For this thought experiment, the dead move slowly and a bite spreads the sickness; those are our rules, not historical facts.
Your goal is smaller than saving Rome: get clear of the crowd, secure water, and reach a place you can defend.
You rent an apartment-like room in an insula, a familiar form of Roman city housing; the height buys time, but a crowded stair can become a trap.
Take your sandals, cloak, water jar, bread, and a small blade; leave valuables and anything you cannot carry at a run.
Do not race toward the Forum or the river port; you would be joining the same crowd looking for food and escape.
Rome depends on grain arriving from distant provinces, so blocked routes can turn a local emergency into a food crisis.
Fill your jar at a public fountain if the route is clear; many residents relied on public basins rather than private taps.
Stay off the main roads once crowds surge; Roman routes connect cities, but travel time depends on terrain, season, and transport.
Leave early and walk toward a nearby farm or small fortified settlement, looking for a well, stored food, and a gate you can watch.

If someone is bitten, tell the group and keep them apart; in our imagined outbreak, hiding it puts everyone at risk.
Ration water, avoid wounds, and do not waste daylight searching for a perfect refuge; a sturdy gate and a clean supply matter more.
News travels by people and animals, not instant signals; Roman communications link distant places, but every message takes time to move.
If you reach a farm before panic, keep a lookout and plan your next move instead of pretending the crisis has ended.
