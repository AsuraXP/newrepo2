You wake up because the light changed, not because an alarm told you to.

For a few seconds you don't move. The ceiling above you is a lattice of reeds, blackened where the smoke goes up through a hole. The ground under your back is packed earth, swept smooth, already warm because the sun is climbing.

Listen. There is no traffic. No refrigerator, no compressor, no distant hum of anything electrical. There are animals, and women talking, and a dog that will not stop.

You lift your hands in front of your face. They are young, and they are filthy, and there is no watch on either wrist.

This is not a ruin. This is a working town, awake before you are.

You have landed somewhere in the ancient Near East, roughly five thousand years before your birth. Nobody here will tell you the year, because nobody here is counting the way you count. There are no clocks, no calendars on a wall, no minutes at all.

The first thing that hits you is the smell. Woodsmoke, wet clay, dung, and something sweet you decide not to investigate.

Then your mouth goes dry, and you learn the first hard lesson. There is no tap. You walk to the canal with a jar on your hip, and the canal is brown. It is downstream of the animals, the laundry, and everybody else.

Boiling that water is the single most valuable thing you know.

Breakfast is barley. The bread is dark, heavy, and full of grit, because it was ground between two stones that shed a little of themselves into the flour. You will feel that grit in every meal for the rest of your life, and you will grind your back teeth nearly flat before you are forty.

There is beer, and it is thick, weak, and faintly sour. It is also safer than the canal, so you drink it the way you used to drink water.

Nothing here keeps. There is no cold, no shelf, no wrap. Salt, smoke, and drying are the only storage you have.

You are also shorter than you were yesterday. You are hungrier than you expected, more or less permanently, and there is a low background dread you cannot place until you realize it is your body thinking about parasites.

Work begins when the light clears the wall. Everyone works. Children carry water, old men mend rope, women brew and weave and grind.

There is no weekend, and no clock to watch. The sun is the shift bell, and it rings twice a day.

Nobody hands you a coin, because there are no coins. You are paid in grain, in beer, in oil and wool. Value is weighed, not printed. Silver is a promise measured on a scale against barley.

Debt is real, and it is personal. You can owe a temple or a household, and if you cannot pay, the debt can reach your children.

You cannot read any of it, and that is normal. Perhaps one person in a hundred can handle the writing, and most of them work for a palace or a temple. So you live on witnesses. A promise is only worth as much as the people who heard it.

If someone wrongs you, there is no police to call. Your family is the police. Your neighbors are the court. The law is less about punishment than about payment, because a town this size cannot survive a feud.

The day ends when the sun does. Light is the only light there is, and when it goes, you go too.

You will not have a room of your own. You will sleep close to people, close to animals, and the dark will be genuinely dark. Some nights you will wake in the middle of it and lie there listening to a town breathe.

A stranger with no household to vouch for him is a problem before he is a guest. Hospitality is not politeness here. It is infrastructure. Everyone's survival depends on the shared assumption that a traveler is worth feeding.

Your status is not something a good interview can change. It is inherited, and it is thin. But skill moves. If you can heal, smith, brew, count, or fix, you eat.

What kills you here is boring. Bad water. An infected cut. A difficult birth. A bad harvest.

What you gain is harder to explain. Quiet. A sky with no light pollution. Sleep that starts when you are tired instead of when you have finished scrolling.

So what would you actually do? Not the heroic version. You would boil water, wash your hands, keep your head down, and make yourself useful fast.

You would not survive by being clever. Cleverness gets people accused of things. You would survive by being useful, quiet, and patient.

And the strangest part is not the dirt, or the beer, or the teeth. It is this. You would know almost exactly what tomorrow's weather is, and almost nothing about tomorrow.

## Production notes

- **POV rule (channel signature):** every frame is seen from the narrator's eyes. Own hands,
  forearms, feet, lap and shadow may appear. No face, ever — not even a reflection.
- Runtime is set by the script, not the other way round. Anchors decide the frame count.
- Facts are stated in ranges ("perhaps one person in a hundred") because the underlying
  figures are estimates. Shekel-as-weight and millstone grit are the load-bearing facts.
- Deliberately excluded: the "two sleeps" theory (early-modern evidence, not ancient
  Near East), any claim that every town had plumbing, and any electricity metaphor.
