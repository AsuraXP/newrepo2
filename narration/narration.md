Imagine you wake in Rome around the year two hundred, in a rented upstairs room above a street filling with the newly dead.
You hear running feet, a crash below, and someone screaming that the dead are walking through the market.
For this thought experiment, they move slowly and a bite spreads the sickness; those rules are fiction, not ancient history.
Your goal is smaller than saving the city: escape the crowd, secure water and food, and find shelter you can defend.
You rent an apartment-like room in an insula, a familiar form of city housing; height buys a little time, but stairs can trap you.

Water is your first practical problem: Rome's aqueducts fed public basins and fountains, but household access was not equal.
Many ordinary Romans fetched water from public fountains, so fill your jar only if you can reach one without crossing a crowd.
If your building has a well or cistern, use it only after checking the entrance and keeping a way out.
Ration what you carry, and remember every safe source; a thirsty group makes poor decisions and leaves shelter too soon.
Food is the next constraint: Rome relied on grain shipped from distant provinces, carried through ports, warehouses, and urban distribution.
That system feeds a city in ordinary times, but any break in ships, roads, or storage could turn panic into scarcity.
So do not join a line at the Forum or river port, where hungry people and frightened families will crowd together.
Instead choose a small group you trust, with someone who knows a route out and someone who can keep track of supplies.
Do not let the strongest person make every decision; one local guide, one calm lookout, and clear rules give everyone a job.
Agree on signals before you leave; a shout may draw danger, while a raised cloth or a knock may keep your group together.
The first streets are the hardest, so stay off broad roads when crowds surge and use side streets only if you can see through them.
A narrow alley hides what is ahead, so do not enter one just because it looks empty; check exits before you commit.
The Roman road network links settlements across the empire, but travel time depends on distance, terrain, season, and your way of moving.
Walking with a load is slow, and a cart may block you; set a modest destination rather than racing for the countryside.
If a gate or bridge is packed, wait at a safe distance and look for another route instead of forcing your way through.
Once you reach the edge of the city, do not mistake open ground for safety; you can see farther, but you are exposed.
A nearby farm or fortified settlement is a better first goal than an unknown mountain; search for water, stored food, and shelter.
Approach from a place where you can watch the yard, listen for movement, and leave again without passing the main entrance.
A closed gate is useful only if the people inside agree to keep it closed, share watch, and protect the water source.
Offer work instead of demanding refuge: carry water, mend a fence, or watch the road, and make yourself useful without crowding the shelter.

Roman soldiers knew how to build temporary camps with ditches and ramparts, but they worked as trained units with tools and orders.
