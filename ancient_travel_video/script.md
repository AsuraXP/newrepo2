You are standing on a beach in East Africa. Sixty thousand years ago. Behind you is the only home your species has ever known. Ahead of you is the entire planet.

Your descendants will walk from this beach to the last tip of South America. The end of the world. More than forty thousand kilometers.

Here is the strange part. If you walked that route without stopping, twenty kilometers a day, every day, it would take about two thousand days. Less than six years of walking.

But your family will take fifty thousand years to do it.

Two thousand days of walking. Fifty thousand years of time. How does that work?

That is the story of the greatest journey ever made. And you are going to walk it.

First, look down at your legs. You are not the fastest animal on the savanna. A kudu can leave you behind in seconds. But you have a trick no antelope has. You can cool down while moving. Sweat. Your whole body is a radiator. A galloping animal has to stop and pant. You just keep jogging.

Your feet have arches that work like springs. Your Achilles tendon stores energy and gives it back. A tiny ligament in your neck keeps your head steady while you run. Chimps do not have it. You do.

In the Kalahari, hunters still prove this works. They chase a kudu in the midday heat, forty degrees Celsius, for two to five hours. Up to thirty five kilometers. The animal sprints, stops, sprints, stops. The hunter just keeps coming. Eventually the kudu cannot run anymore. The hunter walks up to it.

You are not built for speed. You are built for forever.

But here is the secret of the long walk. You never actually go on a journey.

You live in a band of a few dozen people. Every day you walk out from camp to find food. Modern hunter gatherers like the Hadza walk about eight to fourteen kilometers a day. Then you come home.

Every few weeks, camp moves. Eight, ten, twelve kilometers. Maybe twenty five. You pick up everything you own, which is not much, and you walk half a day.

That is it. That is the migration.

You are not crossing a continent. You are just moving camp. Again. And again. For a lifetime. Do the math, and your band drifts less than one kilometer per year. A distance you could cover in ten minutes. That is how fast your family conquers the Earth.

You will never see the destination. Your grandchildren will never see it. Nobody in your family even knows there is a destination. There is only the next waterhole. The next valley. The next season.

So how do you know where to go?

You read the land like a book. The sun rises one way and sets the other. The stars turn around fixed points. Rivers always lead somewhere. Mountains stay put. Coastlines are highways. Walk the beach and the food comes to you. Shellfish, fish, seals.

And when there are no landmarks, you use memory. In Australia, your descendants will build maps out of songs. Songlines. Each verse names a landmark in order. This rock, then that waterhole, then the ridge. Sing the song in the right order, and you can cross hundreds of kilometers of desert you have never seen. The song is the map. The map is the song. Some of these routes run for thousands of kilometers.

No paper. No GPS. Just a melody that tells you where the water is.

Then, about sixty five thousand years ago, your family reaches the edge of Asia. And there is a problem. A big one.

Australia is out there. You cannot see it. But something is. Maybe birds. Maybe a cloud that never moves, the kind that sits over land. And between you and it, open ocean. Fifty to ninety kilometers of it. No land bridge. Ever. Even at the lowest sea levels, Australia was never connected.

So somebody builds a raft. Bamboo, maybe. And a group of people, enough to start a population, gets on it and paddles out of sight of land.

Think about that. The first ocean voyage in human history was not a drift accident. It was a plan. By sixty five to fifty thousand years ago, people are living in Australia. The oldest sites, like Madjedbebe, hold stone tools older than the first human sites in Europe.

Your family crossed the sea before anyone invented the wheel.

Now turn north. The ice age is coming.

Water locks up into glaciers. The oceans drop. One hundred twenty meters. That is a forty story building of missing sea.

And where the Bering Strait should be, between Siberia and Alaska, the seafloor stands up and becomes dry land. Beringia.

It is not a bridge. That is the wrong picture. At its widest it is sixteen hundred kilometers north to south. A country. Grassland, rivers, mammoths, horses. People live there for thousands of years without ever knowing an ocean is underneath their home.

Around twenty thousand years ago, some of your family is standing in America. They did not cross anything. They just walked east, following the food, the way they always do.

Behind them, two ice sheets the size of continents block the way south. But the coast is open. Kelp forests, fish, shellfish, sea mammals. A highway of food running down the Pacific shore. Some people hug the coast in boats. Others wait for a corridor to open between the ice.

And then something changes. The drift becomes a sprint.

Twenty three thousand years ago, kids are leaving footprints in the mud of White Sands, New Mexico. Teenagers, running and playing beside a lake. We can still see their steps today.

Fourteen thousand years ago, your family is in Panama. And then they pour south. Through rainforest, over the Andes, down the spine of two continents.

By about eleven thousand years ago, they reach Tierra del Fuego. The last tip. Cold, wind scoured, staring at Antarctica across the sea. There is no more land. The walk is over.

From the ice to the tip, two whole continents crossed in just a few thousand years. Compared to the fifty thousand year crawl out of Africa, this is a sprint. When the land is empty and rich, your family does not crawl. It runs.

Today, one man is trying to walk the whole route alone. Paul Salopek. He left Ethiopia in twenty thirteen. Forty two thousand kilometers, following your family's path to Tierra del Fuego. He is a professional walker, and he has been at it for over a decade. He is still not done.

One lifetime is not enough. It never was. That is the point.

So the next time you walk to the store and your legs complain, remember what they are. They are the machine that carried your family out of Africa, across the ocean, through the ice age, and down to the end of the world.

Two thousand days of walking. Fifty thousand years of patience. Two thousand generations of just moving camp.

You did not conquer the planet with speed. You conquered it with the next step.

And you are still taking it.
