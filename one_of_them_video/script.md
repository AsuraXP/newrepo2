The dead walk past you on the sidewalk. Shoulder to shoulder. Close enough to touch. One of them bumps your arm and keeps going. It does not stop. It does not snarl. It does not even look. You keep walking. Your heart is hammering, but your face is calm. Three weeks ago, this street would have killed you in ten seconds. Right now, you are the safest person in the world. And you can never tell anyone why.

It started with a pharmacy, three weeks ago, on a street called Fifth. The city was already coming apart. Smoke over the river. Cars packed across the bridge with their doors hanging open. The power was out. The radio was gone. You were down to your last roll of bandages and a bottle of warm water, and somebody had painted the word MEDS on the pharmacy window. You went in through the broken door. The shelves were almost empty. You found painkillers. You found a box of gauze. You did not hear the thing under the counter. It came out fast and low and drove you into the floor. Its teeth went into your forearm like a trap. You got one arm free and grabbed the first thing your hand found. A fire extinguisher. You hit it until it stopped moving. Then you ran. You made it three blocks before your legs gave out. You sat in a doorway and looked at your arm. In the dark, you could see white. Bone. You packed the wound with gauze. You wrapped it in your shirt. And you waited to die.

That is what the bite means. Everyone knows the rules. One scratch. One bite. One drop of blood in the wrong place. The fever takes you in a day. You lock yourself away from the people you love, and you count your hours like coins. You knew the drill. You found a stairwell with a door that locked. You had half a bottle of water and a stolen box of painkillers. You watched the skin around the wound turn red, then purple, then black at the edges. You waited for the fever. You waited for the shaking. You waited for the moment when your thoughts would start to feel borrowed. The first night passed. The second night passed. On the third morning, the swelling was going down. The wound was closing. You felt hungry. You felt tired. You felt alive. That was the first wrong thing. Dead people do not get hungry. Dead people do not check the door twice. Dead people do not whisper their own name, over and over, just to make sure it still sounds like theirs.

You left the stairwell on the fourth morning. The water was gone. Being alive still meant being thirsty. The dead were already in the street. You pressed yourself into the doorway and you counted them. Seven. Eleven. Fifteen. One of them stopped. It turned its head toward you. You stopped breathing. It walked straight at you, and you pressed flat against the wall, and it walked right past. Its hand brushed your sleeve, and it did not slow down. You made a sound. It did not turn. You waved your arms. Nothing. You stepped out into the open, into full daylight, and the dead flowed around you like water around a stone. That is when you understood. The bite did not take you. But it did change you. It put a mask on you that you can never take off. The dead look at you, and they see one of their own.

You roll down your sleeve. You look at the mark. Two crescents of torn skin, almost closed. There is no black at the edges anymore. There is no fever. There is no turning. There is only the mark, and everything it means. The dead will never touch you. And the living can never, ever know. Think about it. Really think. If the survivors knew that a bite could be survived, they would want to know how. If they knew the dead would walk past you like you were furniture. Some would call you a weapon. Some would call you a monster. Some would put you in a cage and take notes while you screamed. So the sleeve stays down. The fear stays on your face. You practice being scared, like everybody else. When the horde comes, you run with the crowd. When the guns fire, you fire too. Every day, you lie. With every breath.

You learn the rules by testing them. Carefully. In dead streets, where no one living can watch. Sound matters. Noise gets a look. A thrown rock turns heads. But a voice, your voice, changes nothing. They ignore your footsteps. They ignore your shadow. If you shove one hard, it notices. If you scream in its face, it just looks confused, and then it goes back to ignoring you. So you keep your hands to yourself. You move slow. You let them bump past you like commuters on a train. And some part of you, the part you will never say out loud, starts to feel at home in the one crowd on earth that would eat anyone else. You test one more rule. The important one. You run. They watch you go. They do not follow. Whatever they see when they look at you, it is not food. It is not even a person. It is just them.

You spend your days alone in the city, and that is the part nobody warns you about. Not the dead. The quiet. You eat cold soup in a laundromat. You talk to yourself out loud, just to hear a living voice. The dead shuffle past the window, and they do not care. One evening, you sit on a rooftop and watch the horde move through the intersection below. Slow and steady. Like a river. And you realize you are not lonely down there. You are lonely up here. That is the night something in you bends.

The convoy finds you a week later. Two pickup trucks and a school bus, welded into armor. Their leader is Hector. Big hands. Quiet voice. He gives you a seat and a can of beans and a job. Carry water. Keep watch. Stay useful. Their medic is June. She takes one look at your arm and says, that looks infected. You say, it is a burn. She frowns. She gives you clean bandages anyway. You change them alone. Always alone, with your back to the door. There is a kid in camp. Ollie. Nine years old. He follows you around like a small, quiet shadow. Days make a rhythm. Water at dawn. Watch at noon. Beans at dark. You learn which floorboards creak. You learn which people ask questions. You keep the sleeve down. You keep the voice steady. You are the calm one now. The brave one. The one who never panics when the dead get close. Everyone admires it. Nobody knows it is not bravery. You like the kid. That is going to be a problem.

You almost tell someone. One night, June is patching a cut on your hand, and she says, you are too calm, are you military. You almost laugh. The truth is right there, behind your teeth. I was bitten three weeks ago. The dead think I am one of them. You say, I just sleep well. She lets it go. You do not let it out. Not ever.

Five days in, the street goes wrong. A runner sprints around the corner, screaming, and the crowd pours in behind him. Everyone is fighting. A dead woman in a green coat gets her hands in Hector's jacket and drags him down. You are right beside her. You grab her shoulder. She lets go of Hector and turns to you. Her hands close on your arm. And then they slide off. Like you are furniture. Like you are nothing. She leans close enough that you can smell her. She looks through you. And then she turns back to the living. Back to Hector, on the ground. You have one second. You drive your knife into the base of her skull before anyone can see that she never wanted you at all. Hector claps your shoulder. Good save. Your hands do not shake until night.

At night, in the bunk, you lie awake and you think about the truth. The dead world is finished with you. The living world would end you. You have one foot in each, and both of them are a lie. Some nights, when the camp is asleep, you walk out past the wire and you stand in the street with them. The dead drift past. You breathe. Nobody is pretending. It is the only time you are not acting. Then you go back. You wash the smell off your hands. You pull the sleeve down.

In the morning, little Ollie sits down next to you at breakfast. Cold beans. Gray light. He eats for a while, and then he says, very quietly, I saw it again yesterday. You say, saw what. The dead lady. In the green coat. He looks up at you. She grabbed everybody. Except you. How come. This is your life now. One of them, to them. One of us, to us. Bitten. Spared. Alone. You look at the mark one more time before the light comes up. Two little crescents. The smallest door in the world, and you live on both sides of it. Tomorrow, you will pull the sleeve down. You will put the fear back on your face. You will carry water, keep watch, stay useful. And you will watch the boy who is watching you, and you will decide what to do about the only living person who is starting to notice. Because the dead have already accepted you. The living are the dangerous ones. And your secret is worth more than your life.
