The stone moves. Blue water bursts through the gate. The crowd cheers.

Then the bell rings.

By sunrise, no one remembers how you moved it—or why they were cheering.

You won't understand why until the next morning.

But first, rewind.

You wake alone on a shelf of black stone beneath a sky with a green ring around the sun. Three pale moons hang in the daylight. Far below, towers lean inward around a dry riverbed. You have no idea how you got here. But you do know what you want: to do something impossible, to make a name that can’t be ignored.

The city is called Orra. You follow the riverbed to its gate, where people are trying to move a stone slab taller than a house. They work in teams. They use ropes, wedges, and a wooden frame. Their tools are clever. Their plan is not.

They push from the wrong side.

You look at the slab’s lower edge. It is caught on a ridge, not locked into the ground. It can turn. All it needs is a pivot—and a longer arm.

“Why don’t they just use a lever?” you wonder.

A woman hears you. “We tried many things,” she says. “At sunset, the bell takes what we learn.”

You think she means it as a warning. Then a child, watching you study the stone, whispers, “Don’t let the Keeper see you think.”

That is when the gate cracks open.

You drag a fallen beam into place and wedge a rounded stone beneath it. The crowd backs away. You put your weight on the long end.

Nothing.

A man laughs. You change the angle, shift the pivot closer to the slab, and ask three workers to steady the beam. You press down again.

The stone shifts a finger’s width.

You ease off before it jams. Clear the grit. Try again, slowly. The slab groans. A line of brilliant blue water escapes, then rushes into the empty canal.

The first surge hits a bend and cracks the wall. You jam a wedge under the beam to hold the gate open just a little. The water slows. The wall holds.

The crowd drops to its knees. The child doesn’t. “You made the stone listen,” she says.

Her name is Tavi. She is already studying the lever, not your face. She points out that the rounded stone should be closer to the beam’s end next time. She is right. You grin. Maybe this world is full of people who can think as quickly as you can—if anyone ever gives them the chance.

You want to explain leverage and friction. Instead, people call you a miracle-worker. For the first time since waking in this world, you feel the future opening in front of you.

At sunset, a bell rings from the highest tower. One low note rolls across the valley. The crowd goes quiet. Then the sound fades, and everyone returns to their homes.

You sleep beside the gate, certain that by morning the whole city will know your name.

The child wakes you.

“Why are you here?” she asks.

She doesn’t remember you. She doesn’t remember the beam, the water, or the miracle. She still knows her name and where she lives, but the events and ideas from yesterday are gone. You remember every detail. You were not born under this world’s sky, and the bell’s pull somehow missed you.

The gate is still open. The canal is still full. But when you point to the lever, she sees only a strange arrangement of wood and stone. Then you notice the chalk marks on the ground. Your diagram is gone. Not smudged. Not washed away. The stone beneath it is smooth.

The city is full of unfinished things: a wheel with one missing spoke, a bridge with marks for a better knot, a kiln with an extra vent that no one remembers adding. These are not signs of people who cannot think. They are clues that someone keeps interrupting the moment an idea becomes progress.

You search the city. A baker can tell you how to keep a fire burning, but not how to improve an oven she built yesterday. A bridge-maker has a dozen clever ways to tie a rope, yet can’t remember the knot he invented at sunset. Everyone is capable. Everyone observes. Everyone has ideas.

You spend the day testing it. Tavi improves a pulley. The bridge-maker invents a knot that can be untied with one hand. A baker changes the shape of an oven vent and gets a hotter, steadier flame. Their ideas are practical, clever, and entirely their own.

At dusk, the bell rings again. By morning, the pulley is still there, the knot still works, and the oven still burns better. But no one remembers inventing them. They look at the improvements as if they appeared by accident. You remember every conversation. You are the only person who can carry yesterday into today.

Every evening, the bell takes the new ones.

The Keeper of Heights summons you to his floating-stone tower. He says the bell protects Orra from dangerous inventions. “People once built things they could not control,” he tells you. “The bell keeps the city safe.”

But his hall is full of devices no one in the city has ever seen: a wheel that turns without wind, a lamp that glows without flame, a tiny model of a bridge with no supports. They are all new ideas, saved here while the people who made them forgot.

The Keeper remembers every one.

He wears a black crystal at his throat. It hums softly when the bell rings. He has not been protecting the city from new ideas. He has been stealing them. Each night, the bell pulls every new discovery from the people and stores it in the crystal. The Keeper keeps the knowledge—and the power that comes with it.

Now he wants you to build a second bell, one that can erase the memory of anyone who questions him. In exchange, he offers you a place beside him. A name the whole world will remember.

It is everything you wanted. And it is the trap.

You pretend to accept. The Keeper gives you until the next sunset. While his guards watch the outer doors, you study the bell’s hanging stone. Its vibration travels through the tower into the black crystal. The crystal is not just storing ideas. It is keeping the bell in tune.

You find the flaw: the whole system rests on one counterweight. Shift it, and the bell’s note will reverse. The stolen ideas will come back—or the crystal will shatter and take them with it.

You have one day to choose.

The child finds you at the gate. She doesn’t remember yesterday, but she has noticed the bell too. You show her the beam, the pivot, and the mark where the stone moved. This time, you don’t tell her to trust you. You let her test it herself.

She sees the slab shift. Her eyes widen. “I knew it,” she says. “I knew I had thought of something.”

Then the Keeper’s guards arrive. They take the beam and carry you to the tower. He is ready for you. The second bell hangs above the city, its black crystal bright with trapped light. All around the valley, people gather for the sunset ringing.

The Keeper offers you one last chance to stand beside him.

You look at the crowd. They are not foolish. They are not helpless. They are people whose best thoughts have been stolen before they could build on them.

You refuse.

The first bell rings. The crystal brightens. The Keeper pulls the lever for the second note.

You throw your weight onto the counterweight.

The tower shudders. The floating stone turns. The bell’s note bends, then reverses. The black crystal begins to glow from the inside—not with one light, but with thousands.

The Keeper grabs for the lever. The child and the builders seize the ropes. You call out the sequence: hold, pull, release. This time, they remember each step long enough to do it together.

The crystal opens like a glass flower.

Across Orra, people stop. A baker remembers the oven she designed. A bridge-maker remembers the knot. The child remembers the lever—and remembers you. Ideas return in flashes: a better canal, a stronger roof, a way to carry water uphill. Not one miracle. A thousand beginnings.

The Keeper’s tower goes silent. His black crystal is gone. For the first time in generations, the bell has nothing to take.

You came here hoping to become a legend. But as the city starts asking questions—how the bell worked, what else can be built, what lies beyond the valley—you realize a name is not the only thing that can last.

Far away, beyond the mountains, another bell answers.

And this time, you know exactly what to look for.
